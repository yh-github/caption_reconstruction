%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
   Recent Video-LLMs typically treat video understanding as a continuous stream of visual encoding. However, real-world events often follow structured, semantic scripts that pre-trained language models can predict without immediate visual evidence. In this work, we investigate the boundary between \textbf{semantic inference} (what must happen) and \textbf{visual perception} (what actually happened) through a novel \textit{Caption Reconstruction} comparative framework. We use two independent methods---a text-based LLM and a Visual-Interpolation baseline method---to reconstruct missing segments from masked videos. Our analysis showcases a spectrum of narrative predictability across video domains: while stochastic events (e.g., nature) require visual grounding, procedural events (e.g., farming, manufacturing) allow text-only models to in-fill accurate reconstructions. This framework serves as a diagnostic tool for measuring multimodal information density and suggests potential for temporal semantic compression in next-generation video architectures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The promise of multimodal AI is the fusion of visual perception with semantic reasoning. Yet, in current Video-LLM architectures, this fusion is often brute-forced: models ingest massive sequences of visual tokens regardless of the information content. This approach ignores a fundamental property of the physical world: narrative predictability.

Consider a video of a person chopping an onion. If the next scene is missing, a human (or an LLM) can predict with high confidence that the onion was fried, the peppers were added, and the dish was stirred. The exact visual details are \textit{stochastic residuals}, but the \textit{semantic action} is procedurally deterministic. We propose a \textbf{Comparative Reconstruction Framework} to quantify this ``Semantic Gap'' between text-based inference and visual-only interpolation.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We represent a video $V$ as a sequence of $T$ semantic units, where each unit $u_t$ consists of a visual embedding $v_t$ and a textual caption $c_t$. The task is to mask a subset of these units $M \subset \{1 \dots T\}$ and reconstruct the missing semantic content. Our target is the \textbf{semantic embedding} of the missing segment.

\subsection{Comparative Pathways}
\textbf{Text Logic:} Tests the limit of \textbf{Semantic Predictability}. It receives observed segments $C\textSub{obs} = \{(t, c_t) \mid t \notin M\}$ and employs an LLM to predict the text $\hat{c}_t$, which is then encoded: $\hat{e}\textSub{text} = \text{Encoder}(\hat{c}_t)$.

\textbf{Visual Continuity:} Tests \textbf{Visual Fidelity}. It receives visual embeddings $V\textSub{obs} = \{v_t \mid t \notin M\}$ and reconstructs $\hat{e}\textSub{vis}$ via non-parametric weighted averaging of neighboring vectors.

\subsection{Evaluation: Predictability Delta}
To normalize across different embedding densities, we define the \textbf{Predictability Delta ($\deltaMetric$)}:
\begin{equation}
    \deltaMetric = \text{Rank}(\hat{e}\textSub{text}) - \text{Rank}(\hat{e}\textSub{vis})
\end{equation}
where $\deltaMetric \ll 0$ indicates \textbf{Semantic Dominance} (predictable logic) and $\deltaMetric \gg 0$ indicates \textbf{Visual Necessity} (stochastic change).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate on a dataset derived from WildQA, spanning 100 diverse videos. 

\subsection{Domain Analysis}
\textbf{Procedural Resilience:} Categories like \textit{Farming} and \textit{Military} exhibit instances of extreme semantic dominance ($\deltaMetric < -50$). The LLM successfully reconstructs intermediate steps even when the visual jump is large.

\textbf{Stochastic Dependence:} \textit{Nature} and \textit{Scenery} show a heavy tail toward visual necessity ($\deltaMetric > 50$), as low-level physical changes are hard to describe textually.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our findings support the view that VLMs suffer from information redundancy. For procedural content, the visual stream is often semantically redundant. Future work will investigate the ``Event Horizon''---the point at which even procedural scripts become chaotic and both models fail.

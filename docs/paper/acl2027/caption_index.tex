\title{Caption Once, Ask Cheaply: What a Question-Blind Caption Index\\Buys a Video Reader}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Video QA systems can caption a video once, without the question, and answer later questions from this caption index with a cheap text model. We ask why such an index can beat the frames it was made from, and what it saves. Because we give the captioner exactly the reader's frames, the captions hold nothing the frames lack, and we can split their effect into source, visual budget, captioner and modality. Who writes the captions matters most. In a pre-registered test on TempCompass, Gemini's captions of Claude Haiku's frames raise Haiku's accuracy by 0.15 over Haiku's own captions, and in follow-ups predicted before they ran, the gain recurs at matched visual tokens, with Sonnet as captioner, and with an open-weights reader. No reader gains measurably from its own captions over its frames. The saving comes from a cheaper reader. Haiku reading a stronger model's index costs 1/23 to 1/26 as much per question as Sonnet reading the frames, repays the captioning within four questions per video, and loses at most 9 points. Most wrong answers come from captions that leave the question undecided. Where index and frames disagree, the question's aspect and the models moderately predict which one is right.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many video question answering systems first caption the video and then reason over the text \citep{llovi,videotree,silvr}. If the captions are written once, before any question is known, they form an index that every later question can reuse. Captioning is then paid once per video, and each question costs only a text read. Recent work builds such indexes for long videos \citep{cfd,capmem,sprout}. In CapMem, a model's own captions beat its frames only on videos longer than 20 minutes.

Two questions about such an index are still open. First, why would a reader answer better from captions than from the frames they describe? The captioner may have seen more of the video, spent more visual tokens or simply been a stronger model, or the reader may find text easier than pixels. Second, does the index pay for itself? That depends on the whole cost of each question, and on the one-time cost of captioning.

We hold the information fixed by giving the captioner exactly the frames the reader would see. A caption made from these frames cannot contain information about the video that the frames lack (Proposition~1), so whatever accuracy the captions add is information the reader can use more easily, not new information. We then change one thing at a time. This splits the effect of captions into four parts: the source the captioner sees (the video or the reader's frames), the visual tokens it spends, the captioner model, and the modality (a reader's self-captions against its frames). Two Claude models, Haiku and Sonnet, read. Haiku, Sonnet and Gemini write captions, and an open-weights model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, reads the main arms and captions for itself. The 297 multiple-choice questions come from TempCompass \citep{tempcompass}, on videos with a median length of 10\,s. We set the main test on a separate development set and pre-registered it, and we wrote down a predicted value for each follow-up before running it.

We find that the captioner matters and the modality does not. With Haiku reading, Gemini's captions of the reader's frames beat Haiku's self-captions by 0.15 in the pre-registered test. The gain holds when both captioners spend matched visual tokens, and when Sonnet captions input identical to Haiku's. Self-captions buy little. Haiku is no more accurate on its own than on the frames they describe, Sonnet is less accurate on its own, and the open-weights reader replicates both results. Two of our pre-registered predictions failed. We expected Haiku to lose on its self-captions, and captions to lose direction and speed more than other aspects. The index pays through a cheaper reader. On Sonnet's captions, Haiku and Sonnet are within 0.02 of each other, although Sonnet is 0.19 more accurate on the frames. Per question, Haiku on a stronger captioner's index costs 1/23 to 1/26 of Sonnet on the frames, for 0 to 9 points of accuracy.

Most of what captions lose, they lose on videos that TempCompass built as stress tests. On unmanipulated videos, Sonnet on its self-captions is within 0.01 of its frames. When two copies of a clip play at different speeds, the question-blind captions do not say which copy is faster, although the same model often sees the difference once it is shown the question, and captions written with the question in view recover most of this gap. On reversed clips the captions describe the forward event or commit to no direction, and writing them with the question helps little. Finally, we ask when to read the index and when the frames. Where the two disagree, the question's aspect and the pair of models predict which one is right (AUC 0.67 on new questions about the same videos, for a rule frozen beforehand), but choosing by them gains little accuracy. Scores of the video's motion, its cuts or how well the captions cover the frames add nothing.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A decomposition of the effect of captions into source, visual budget, captioner and modality effects, with arms that change one thing each, in two model families (\S\ref{sec:effects}).
\item A pre-registered test and predicted replications, at identical input and with an open-weights reader, that show a positive captioner effect and a modality effect of at most +0.09 (\S\ref{sec:captioner}--\ref{sec:modality}).
\item A cost account per question that counts input, output and thinking tokens, with the break-even number of questions per video, also projected to longer videos (\S\ref{sec:cost}).
\item An account of what question-blind captions lose, from minimal pairs, question-aware controls and an audit of wrong answers (\S\ref{sec:types}--\ref{sec:aware}).
\item A decision rule for reading the index or the frames, frozen and tested on 738 new questions, and a null result for scores of the video and its captions as predictors (\S\ref{sec:decide}).
\end{enumerate}
We study only short videos, multiple-choice questions and one benchmark.

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Task and data}
\label{sec:data}
We use TempCompass's multiple-choice questions \citep{tempcompass} on five aspects: action, speed, direction, order and attribute change. The hypotheses come from a development set of 100 questions. The confirmatory set samples 60 questions per aspect, one per video, on videos the development set does not use, and 297 remain after three exclusions (Appendix~\ref{app:amend}). TempCompass builds most of its videos to defeat shortcuts, so we report four video types: plain (137), reversed (74), stacked (41) and two-clip (45). A stacked video shows one clip twice, one copy above the other, at two speeds. The type nearly determines the aspect. Many videos share a filmed clip with others, so the questions fall into 156 clusters. We split each video into 5\,s windows and take one frame per second at 224\,px on the long edge (40 Claude image tokens each). The videos are short (median 9.7\,s), and 58\% have one or two windows. We keep the per-window design anyway, because it scales to long video and one caption per video does no better (\S\ref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Arms}
\label{sec:arms}

An arm gives the reader the question and options and, for each window, one kind of evidence: nothing (question only), the middle frame, all 5 frames, or one caption of those frames. Table~\ref{tab:arms} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} lists every arm with its visual tokens. The readers are Claude Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 (pinned model IDs, effort medium), called through \texttt{claude -p} with no tools and a fresh session per call. As an open-weights check, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen25vl} (float16, greedy decoding) reads the main arms (\S\ref{sec:robust}). Haiku, Sonnet, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen write captions. We call Sonnet and Gemini \emph{stronger} captioners than Haiku. For Sonnet this rests on reading the same frames better (0.798 against Haiku's 0.613). Gemini never reads, so for Gemini it rests only on how useful its captions are (\S\ref{sec:captioner}). A reader's \emph{self-captions} are its own captions of the frames it would read. Every caption is question-blind and sees only its window, with two exceptions: the whole-video captions see all of a video's frames (\S\ref{sec:robust}), and the question-aware captions see the question (\S\ref{sec:aware}). All captioners get the same prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\subsection{Statistics and pre-registration}
\label{sec:stats}
We set the main test, P1--P3, on the development set and pre-registered it: we wrote it down with its analysis code and committed both to the repository before any confirmatory call. It carries 98.33\% CIs (Bonferroni over three). Every other comparison is exploratory. We wrote each one down with a predicted value and committed it before its first call, ran its analysis once, and report it with a 95\% CI. Where a result was not predicted, we say so. The two tests of the decision rule (\S\ref{sec:decide}) each have two primary hypotheses, at 97.5\% (Bonferroni over two), and Appendix~\ref{app:dev} lists every term with its prediction. CIs come from a paired bootstrap over questions. A ``\cl'' marks a CI that resamples the 156 clip clusters instead, and the two rarely disagree (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). The repository's full history, which holds each prediction in a commit dated before its run, is archived by Software Heritage (Appendix~\ref{app:release}).

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
\textbf{Proposition~1 (garbling).} Let $F$ be the reader's frames, $Q$ the question and $A$ its answer. A question-blind caption $C$ of the frames is drawn by the captioner from $F$ alone, so any answering rule that reads $(Q, C)$ can be run on $(Q, F)$ by first drawing $C$. Hence no rule on the caption is more accurate than the best rule on the frames, and $I(A; C \mid Q) \le I(A; F \mid Q)$ by the data-processing inequality \citep{cover2006}. A bounded reader can still extract more \emph{usable} information \citep{xu2020usable} from a good caption than from the pixels. The captioner and modality effects measure such differences.

\textbf{The four effects.}\label{sec:effects} Each effect compares two arms that differ in one thing, with Haiku reading (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). \emph{Source}: Gemini's captions of the video window against its captions of the reader's frames played as a 1\,fps clip. Gemini samples the video at 1\,fps too, at the same instants, so the source effect measures what the original file adds at those instants (mostly resolution), not motion between them. \emph{Visual budget}: one captioner's captions of the same frames at two visual-token counts. \emph{Captioner}: two captioners' captions of the same frames at equal or matched visual tokens. \emph{Modality}: the reader on its self-captions against the reader on those frames (the two prompts also differ in the sentence that says what the evidence is; Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). In the Claude chain, source and visual budget are zero by design. In the Gemini chain both branch from the frame-clip captions, so the terms add up along two paths (Figure~\ref{fig:design}).

\textbf{Proposition~2 (break-even).} An index $\tau$ that costs $C(\tau)$ per video and $c_\tau$ per question pays for itself, against frames at $c_f$ per question, after
\[ \qbe = \frac{C(\tau)}{c_f - c_\tau} \]
questions per video, the break-even quantity. With a reader downgrade, $c_\tau$ is the cheaper reader's cost. If each of $W$ windows is captioned in its own call at cost $k$, and costs the reader $s_f$ as frames and $s_\tau$ as text, then $\qbe \to k / (s_f - s_\tau)$ as $W$ grows, so on long videos the break-even is set by the per-window prices and no longer by the fixed prompt.

\textbf{Omission.} Suppose the same model answers correctly from the frames with the question in view, but its question-blind captions do not decide the question. Then the captioner could see the fact and did not write it down, and we call the loss an omission.

\textbf{Retention}, (captions − question only) / (frames − question only), is the share of the frames' gain over the question alone that an index keeps.

\subsection{Who writes the caption matters}
\label{sec:captioner}

We test the captioner first, in the pre-registered P3. With Haiku reading, Gemini's captions of exactly the reader's frames beat Haiku's self-captions by +0.148 [+0.088, +0.209], 2.5 times the +0.06 we predicted. The gain is positive in every aspect (+0.10 to +0.20) and in every video type (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

P3 has a catch. Its two captioners spend different numbers of visual tokens on the same pixels. Gemini's image path encodes each 224\,px frame at ≈ 1,100 tokens, or ≈ 5,500 per window, where Haiku spends 200. We therefore also had Gemini caption the same frames as a 1\,fps clip, through its video path (≈ 280--330 tokens per window, as in the video arm). This splits P3 into two parts of similar size, a captioner effect of +0.081 [+0.030, +0.131] at about matched visual tokens and a visual budget effect of +0.067 (Table~\ref{tab:effects} in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). Within Claude, we found no such budget gain in the range we tested. Sonnet's captions of 448\,px frames take 3.6 times the visual tokens and are no more useful to Haiku (+0.003; Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

The source effect is near zero (+0.013 [−0.030, +0.061]). Gemini samples the video window at the same 1\,fps instants and reduces each frame to ≈ 66 tokens. Little of the original file's resolution thus reaches the captioner, and nothing between the instants does. All four of our predictions for this split were within 0.02 of the results.

Within one model family, we can match the captioner's input exactly. Sonnet captions the same five JPEGs per window, with the same prompt and effort as Haiku's self-captions, and in each of the 876 windows its caption call took exactly 2 more input tokens than Haiku's. Only the captioning model differs. Haiku reads Sonnet's captions more accurately than its self-captions, by \textbf{+0.064 [+0.017, +0.111]}, and the predicted +0.09 lies inside the CI. The effect holds on minimal pairs, where a guess cannot score (+0.104 in pair accuracy; \S\ref{sec:types}). It holds on 738 further questions about the same videos (+0.072; \S\ref{sec:decide}), and it holds with Qwen reading its self-captions and Sonnet's (+0.125; \S\ref{sec:robust}). Sonnet's captions are not measurably more useful to Haiku than Gemini's at about equal visual tokens (Sonnet − Gemini frame-clip, −0.017 [−0.071, +0.037]).

\subsection{Text itself is not easier for the reader}
\label{sec:modality}

The modality effect asks whether a reader does better on its own words than on the pixels they came from. For Haiku this is the pre-registered P2, self-captions − 5 frames, and it comes out at +0.030 [−0.034, +0.094]. The CI excludes both our prediction (−0.08) and any gain above +0.09. On 738 further questions about the same videos the effect is −0.064 (predicted +0.03), because Haiku's frames score higher there, mostly on direction (\S\ref{sec:decide}). The sign for Haiku thus depends on the questions, and neither set shows a gain from its own words. Sonnet and Qwen lose accuracy on their self-captions. For Sonnet the effect is −0.088 [−0.135, −0.044], or −0.067 when it captions the 448\,px frames, and for Qwen it is −0.121 (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). So the modality effect is near 0 only for Haiku, the reader with the lowest point estimate on frames (Qwen − Haiku +0.044 [−0.010, +0.101]; Table~\ref{tab:variants}).

The readers tie on Sonnet's captions. From Haiku to Sonnet, accuracy on frames rises from 0.613 to 0.798, but accuracy on Sonnet's captions stays flat (0.707 → 0.710; Sonnet − Haiku +0.003 [−0.040, +0.047]; Figure~\ref{fig:readers} in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). On these captions, a reader that costs 17× less per question is not measurably less accurate. The same holds on all four indexes Sonnet wrote (Sonnet − Haiku +0.000 to +0.020, every CI includes 0; Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). One reading is that the captions hold a fixed amount of usable information, between what the two readers get from the frames. Haiku on frames is below it and gains from the captions, and Sonnet is above it and loses. A fixed compressed text narrows the gap between readers in retrieval-augmented QA too \citep{compressneutral}.

Could the tie come from Sonnet reading its own words? To check, we predicted the readers' gap on Haiku's self-captions and had Sonnet read them. Sonnet is not measurably better than Haiku on these either (+0.034 [−0.010, +0.081]), and the two gaps do not differ measurably (−0.030). The tie therefore does not come from Sonnet's own words, although we cannot exclude an advantage of up to 0.08 on Haiku's captions.

Not every index ties. Sonnet reads Gemini's video and frame-clip captions more accurately than Haiku does (+0.054 and +0.064), and on Gemini's frame captions it is level with its own frames (0.811 against 0.798). Qwen trails Haiku on every shared index (−0.04 to −0.08), mostly on two-clip videos (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). Haiku's gain from captions is not an artefact of small frames either, since captions still beat 448\,px frames at 1.76× the input tokens (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

\subsection{Where captions lose: video types and minimal pairs}
\label{sec:types}

Built videos (reversed, stacked and two-clip) make up 54\% of the confirmatory set. Every stacked question is about speed and every two-clip question about order, so we report results by type (Figure~\ref{fig:types} in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). On plain videos, the five arms lie within 0.09 of each other (0.80--0.89). On the built videos they separate.

From accuracy on one video, we cannot tell a representation that lost the fact from a reader that guessed. Minimal pairs can. We ask each of 193 confirmatory questions again, with the same wording and option order, on a sibling video that is built from the same filmed clip and has a different correct option. A pair is the plain and the reversed version of a clip (107 pairs), two stacked versions at different speeds (41), or two arrangements of the same two clips (45). An arm that does not receive the fact cannot tell the two videos apart, and if it gives the same answer on both, it gets at most one right. Table~\ref{tab:pairs} reports pair accuracy, the share of pairs answered correctly on both sides (chance 0.093), and the same-answer rate.

\paragraph{Captions help a weak reader on two-clip videos.} To answer from frames, Haiku must keep about ten small frames from two clips apart. On two-clip pairs it is near chance (0.18). Its self-captions raise its pair accuracy to 0.53, a modality effect of +0.36, and on Sonnet's captions its accuracy on two-clip videos is 0.33 above its frames. We predicted both gains.

\paragraph{On stacked videos, question-blind captions drop the comparison.} Sonnet on frames answers 0.51 of stacked pairs correctly on both sides. Every caption arm scores 0.10--0.20, near pair chance, and that includes Sonnet on its self-captions (0.10; −0.41 against its frames). For a man jumping rope in two copies (answer: the one above is faster), Gemini writes ``An athletic man \ldots{} jumps rope in a split-screen video''. Sonnet often sees the difference when it looks with the question in view, and loses it when it writes without the question. Much of the loss is thus an omission (\S\ref{sec:theory}), and captions written with the question in view recover most of it (\S\ref{sec:aware}).

\paragraph{On reversed videos, captions narrate forward or do not commit.} The question points to the forward event, so from the question alone both readers score below chance on reversed videos (Haiku 0.16, Sonnet 0.18). Sonnet on frames recovers part of the reversal (0.64). Its question-blind captions keep little of it (−0.18 against its frames), and its question-aware captions barely more (−0.15; \S\ref{sec:aware}). In one clip a pile of salt is played backwards (answer: shrinking), and all five of Gemini's video captions describe salt pouring onto the pile. Split by type, the loss from writing blind sits on reversed and stacked videos. On plain and two-clip videos, Sonnet's captions keep 0.95--1.00 of its frames' gain (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}).

\subsection{Why caption arms fail: an audit}
\label{sec:audit}

We audited every caption set and question that a reader got wrong in one of seven reader arms (442 items). A judge (Sonnet) saw the captions, question, options and correct answer. It labelled the captions as stating the correct option (a reader error), a wrong option (a misstatement), or neither (an omission), and quoted the caption as evidence (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). Omissions dominate. In every arm, 0.61--0.73 of the wrong answers come from captions that do not decide the question, and reader errors are only 0.05--0.17 (Table~\ref{tab:audit} in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). No reader can read a fact that the index lacks, which fits the readers' tie on Sonnet's captions (\S\ref{sec:modality}). The wrong answers also concentrate on built videos, which hold 54\% of the questions but 0.74--0.84 of each arm's wrong answers. To check the judge, one author labelled 40 items without seeing its labels. The two agree at κ = 0.81 [0.64, 0.96].

\subsection{From question-blind to question-aware}
\label{sec:aware}

Three further arms move the description toward the question, each with new captions (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). The first reads the frames at every question, so it is not an index. Haiku sees the frames and the question and \emph{describes, then answers} (one sentence per window, then the answer, in one call). It scores 0.684, +0.071 over the frames arm, and on stacked videos it beats its question-blind self-captions by \textbf{+0.22 [+0.09, +0.37]}.

The second arm asks whether text alone can carry what blind captions omit. Its \emph{question-aware captions} are written from the same 5 frames, with one added bullet that gives the question and options, and are read without the frames. Overall they gain little over the blind captions (+0.017 for Haiku and for Sonnet, each reading its own; predicted +0.04 and +0.05). Stacked videos are the exception. There Haiku gains +0.22 and Sonnet's gap to its frames shrinks from −0.27 to −0.10, while on reversed videos the gap barely moves (−0.18 to −0.15). Text written with the question thus carries most of the stacked comparison, but the reversal is lost even when the captioner knows the question.

In the third arm, the added bullet names only the five kinds of question. With Sonnet reading, these aspect-aware captions gain +0.040 [+0.003, +0.081] over Sonnet's whole-video captions, and with Haiku reading +0.027. On stacked videos, Sonnet on frames stays 0.27 ahead.

\subsection{When to read the index: a decision rule}
\label{sec:decide}

A system that keeps both the index and the frames could choose between them per question. A reader's answers from the two disagree on 20\% of questions, and either can be right. In three logged steps (Appendix~\ref{app:decide}), we asked whether anything known before either answer is read says which will be right.

\paragraph{Scores of the video and its captions do not predict.} We first asked whether two scores predict a \emph{loss}, a reader right on its frames and wrong on its self-captions, within each video type. \emph{Motion} is the mean SigLIP~2 distance between consecutive frames \citep{siglip2}, and \emph{coverage} the SigLIP~2 similarity of each caption to its window's frames. Neither predicts a loss. The stratified AUC is 0.42 [0.28, 0.57] for motion (predicted 0.60) and 0.53 [0.38, 0.67] for coverage (predicted 0.58; 97.5\% CIs over clip clusters).

\paragraph{A rule from the aspect and the models.} In a development run, we fit logistic models to the 898 disagreements of 15 reader--index pairs. The question's aspect and the pair (reader, captioner family) give an out-of-fold AUC of 0.756. Adding motion, cuts, the number of windows, coverage, or how often the captions use the aspect's words (``faster'', ``leftward'', ``then'') raises it by at most 0.002 (Table~\ref{tab:a7}). We froze this rule and tested it on the 738 TempCompass questions about the confirmatory videos that the confirmatory set does not ask (2,952 Haiku calls). In words, the rule reads Gemini's captions, and with Claude's captions it reads the frames on speed questions. On the 492 disagreements its AUC is \textbf{0.666} [0.587, 0.743] (97.5\%, \cl; predicted 0.68), so the first primary test passes. The second, a shift toward the frames on speed questions, narrowly fails (−0.148 [−0.320, +0.007]; predicted −0.25). The rule beats always reading the index by +0.013, \cl{} [+0.002, +0.026], but not always reading the frames (+0.014). The test does reuse the confirmatory videos. On the 67 disagreements about an aspect the confirmatory set had not asked of the video, the rule does not hold (AUC 0.40, descriptive). On long video, \citet{readfirst} instead look at the frames when two text answers disagree, a signal our rule does not use.

\subsection{Robustness checks and nulls}
\label{sec:robust}

Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct reads eight arms and captions every window from exactly the 5 frames. Its self-captions are the weakest in the study, even for Qwen itself, and Gemini's video captions add only +0.040 over its frames (predicted +0.12; Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). One Sonnet caption per video instead of one per window does no better (+0.013 with Haiku reading, +0.010 with Sonnet), at half the index cost. Captions are noisy. Re-captioning identical input changes 10--17\% of answers, so comparisons between caption arms, and by type, carry this noise too. When Haiku re-read its own self-captions 13 days later, 10.8\% of its answers changed, but its accuracy did not change measurably (−0.013). Finally, the pre-registered aspect contrast (P1) did not pass. At −0.101 [−0.231, +0.028] for Sonnet, it is half the predicted −0.20.

\section{Cost and break-even}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Accounting.} We price every call at list price: Claude Haiku 5.5 at \$0.10 / \$0.50 and Sonnet 5.5 at \$2 / \$10 per million input / output tokens, with thinking billed as output (this equals the CLI's reported cost on every call), and Gemini captioning at \$0.50 / \$3.00. \qbe{} follows Proposition~2, with $c$ a reader's mean cost over the 297 questions and $C(\tau)$ the cost of captioning all of a video's windows once. Its CIs resample questions, and thereby videos, since each video has one question.

\paragraph{For one reader, the index saves little.} Against 5 frames, Gemini video captions save 18\% per question for Haiku and 30\% for Sonnet. That is little, because the fixed reader prompt (≈ 1,025 tokens) dominates the input (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). Sonnet on Gemini video captions still repays after $\qbe = 5.8$ [5.2, 6.5] questions, with no measurable accuracy difference (−0.007). For Haiku, which is 0.125 more accurate on these captions than on its frames, the index repays only after 202 [156, 279].

\paragraph{The reader downgrade pays.} Per question, Haiku on any index written by a stronger captioner costs 1/23 to 1/26 of Sonnet on frames (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The index repays its captioning within 1.2--3.7 questions per video, 1.2--1.3 for the whole-video indexes and 1.8--3.7 for the per-window ones (Table~\ref{tab:qbe} in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). At 10 questions per video, every index Haiku reads costs less than half of Sonnet on frames, captioning included (Figure~\ref{fig:cost} in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). What the downgrade costs in accuracy depends on the index. Gemini frame captions bring Haiku within 0.01 of Sonnet on frames (−0.007), but they are the most expensive index (\$0.0146 per video). In the Claude chain, Sonnet captions once and Haiku answers. There the downgrade costs −0.091 [−0.145, −0.040] at $\qbe = 2.3$, and aspect-aware captions reduce this to −0.051 at $\qbe = 1.3$. Our costs include ≈ 850 input tokens that the CLI adds to every Claude call, which an API deployment would drop or cache. Without them, the reader downgrade costs 1/25 to 1/34 of Sonnet on frames per question and repays within 1.4--6.3 questions per video (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

\paragraph{Longer videos.} We also project the costs, by Proposition~2, to longer videos whose every window is read (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). As videos grow, captions save more per question, but \qbe{} does not fall, because captioning grows with every window too. For the reader downgrade, \qbe{} rises from 1.8--3.7 to 4.5--8.9 at one minute. We checked the projection once, on 39 three-minute EgoSchema videos (36 windows) from a development run. With their larger frames counted, the measured costs per question are 1.04--1.11 times the projected ones, and the measured \qbe{} 0.93--1.00 times.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Caption, then reason.} Composing models through language goes back to Socratic Models \citep{socratic}, and many video QA systems caption first and reason over the text \citep{llovi,videotree,silvr,vamos}, or let an LLM agent choose which frames to caption \citep{videoagent}. LLoVi already finds that the captioner matters. Caption indexes and memories reuse the captions across questions \citep{cfd,capmem,sprout,vidctx}. Caption utility is also judged by QA rather than by similarity \citep{captionqa,capquiz,visil}, and CapRL \citep{caprl} trains a captioner on it. Prism \citep{prism} and MetaCaptioner \citep{metacaptioner} vary the captioner in front of a fixed reader. CapMem \citep{capmem} and REEL \citep{reel} are the closest work. On long egocentric video, CapMem's Qwen readers read the captions of six Qwen captioners of the same frames, and their own, but it does not separate source from visual budget, cross model families or price a cheaper reader. In CapMem the stronger reader gains on fixed captions. On Sonnet's captions we find no such gain, and on Gemini's a small one. REEL gives a model's own question-blind text record and its cached visual states exactly the frames its frame path sees, on long video with six open models, and the text record changes 19.7--28.7\% of answers at nearly equal mean accuracy. One model writes and reads, so REEL has no captioner swap and no cheaper reader. Our control extends theirs to a captioner other than the reader.

\textbf{Text or pixels.} IsoBench \citep{isobench} finds that models score 15--29 points higher on exact text renderings of images than on the images, and TokenSwap \citep{tokenswap} studies the same contrast. When a model reads its own description, the sign varies. Own captions give a weak 7B model +22 to +29 points on synthetic image tasks \citep{captionthis}, while appending the model's own caption costs about 10 points on HallusionBench \citep{geass}, and a one-call ``state first'' prompt does not help on charts \citep{seeingsolving}. On Visual7W, each of seven open models is less accurate on its own question-blind caption alone than on the caption with the image, by 11.3 points on average \citep{revisitcaption}. Our modality effect (at most +0.09 for Haiku, negative for Sonnet and Qwen) has CapMem's sign for videos under 20 minutes.

\textbf{Readers on a fixed text.} In retrieval-augmented QA, when every reader gets the same compressed text per question, the gap between the weakest and the strongest reader on HotpotQA shrinks from 31.8 to 7.8 points with one compressor, and weaker readers gain more \citep{compressneutral}. Our readers' tie is the same effect on a video index.

\textbf{Temporal video, routing and cost.} Our minimal pairs follow TempCompass's conflicting videos \citep{tempcompass}, MotionBlind \citep{motionblind} and TimeBlind \citep{timeblind}, but we apply them to representations instead of models. Video models often fail direction, speed and reversal from pixels too \citep{whichway,arrowoftime,reversaldrop,t3}. Cascades and routers send each query to a cheaper model when they can \citep{frugalgpt,routellm,safecascade}, and an index opens that cheap route for video. We found no paper that solves for the break-even with a reader downgrade. Appendix~\ref{app:related} covers further work on routing, on what an index keeps, and on cost.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
We gave each captioner exactly the reader's frames. A stronger captioner's question-blind captions then make Claude Haiku and an open 7B reader more accurate, and no reader gains measurably from its self-captions (at most +0.09, for Haiku). An index should therefore be written by a stronger model than its reader. The saving comes from the reader downgrade. With the same reader, an index saves only 18--30\% per question. A cheaper reader, within 0.02 of Sonnet on Sonnet's captions, cuts the cost by a factor of 23--26 and repays the captioning within a few questions per video. A blind index loses what the captioner does not write without the question, namely comparisons between two copies of a clip and reversals, so a system should keep the frames for such questions. Which of the two to read is only moderately predictable, from the question's aspect and the models (AUC 0.67 on new questions about the same videos), and scores of the video and its captions add nothing. The control arms matter beyond this study. Without self-caption, frames and question-only arms, a ``caption effect'' would mix the captioner, the modality and the prior, since the same captions help Haiku and cost Sonnet (retention 1.47 and 0.77).

\section*{Limitations}
\textbf{Scope.} We test one benchmark, short clips (median 9.7\,s) and multiple-choice questions. Of the confirmatory videos, 54\% are manipulated (reversed, stacked, two-clip), so overall accuracies mix natural video with stress tests. We report plain videos separately (Table~\ref{tab:headroom}). The costs for longer videos (\S\ref{sec:cost}) are projections from short clips, for a reader that reads every window. We checked them once, on 3-minute videos, where they held within 11\% once frame size was counted. All captions except the question-aware ones (\S\ref{sec:aware}) follow one generic, question-blind prompt that does not mention the benchmark's manipulations. Captions written for retrieval or question answering may keep more.

\textbf{Contamination.} TempCompass (March 2024) predates every reader's training cutoff (June 2026 for the Claude models), so the readers may have seen its questions. The question alone scores 0.40--0.41, 0.11 above chance, but the excess comes from 100 of the 297 questions. For 197 of the 297 confirmatory questions, TempCompass itself asks the same question, with the same options, of another video with another answer, so a remembered answer cannot fit both. On these 197 the question alone is at chance (Haiku +0.02, Sonnet $-$0.01), and the headline effects keep their signs: captioner +0.08 [+0.02, +0.14], the readers' tie on Sonnet's captions (Haiku − Sonnet) +0.02 [$-$0.04, +0.07], Haiku on Sonnet's captions vs.\ Sonnet on frames $-$0.11 [$-$0.18, $-$0.04]. The captioner effect and the downgrade's cost are larger there than on the other 100. On minimal-pair accuracy, which a text-only answer cannot earn, they hold too (Table~\ref{tab:pairs}; the tie is 0.00 [$-$0.07, +0.06]). We did not extend the study to EgoSchema \citep{egoschema}, because in a development run Sonnet scored 0.72 from the question alone (chance 0.20; 39 questions). A confirmatory EgoSchema run, logged in advance, was stopped for this reason 53 minutes after it started, before any of its results were computed.

\textbf{Readers.} The closed readers run through a CLI, so its system prompt is part of every input and temperature is not controlled. The CLI's built-in prompt changed between the first runs (October 18--20) and the last (October 31). A re-read of Haiku's self-captions across that gap changed accuracy by −0.013 [−0.047, +0.020] (\S\ref{sec:robust}). Costs are at list price for these calls, and \S\ref{sec:cost} also reports them without the CLI's prompt. The model decides per call whether to think (effort medium), so we observe thinking but do not control it. Our mitigations are pinned model IDs, isolation checked on every call, released outputs, and an open-weights reader with greedy decoding that replicates the captioner and modality results (\S\ref{sec:robust}). That reader is one 7B model, with its own default system prompt. In the main chain Claude reads Claude's captions, and the Gemini chain and the Qwen reader check this. Haiku's accuracy on frames dropped from the development to the confirmatory set (0.70 to 0.61), and about half of the drop is the different mix of video types.

\textbf{Inference.} Questions share filmed clips, so we also report CIs over clip clusters. Most claims beyond the pre-registered test are exploratory. We predicted each follow-up in writing before it ran, but not the readers' tie on Sonnet's captions, the thinking pattern, the analysis by video type of the first two runs, or the checks marked as computed after their run. The audit's judge (Sonnet) also wrote one of the caption sets it judges, and 40 blind hand labels check it (κ = 0.81). We tested the decision rule (\S\ref{sec:decide}) on new questions about the confirmatory videos, not on new videos. Of the 738, 618 ask the same aspect of a video as a confirmatory question, mostly reworded, so the test is closer to a replication than to an independent sample. On the same videos and with the same number of options, the new direction questions moved Haiku's accuracy on frames by 0.20 (Appendix~\ref{app:decide}). Accuracies for one reader thus depend on how the questions are worded as well as on the videos.

\textbf{Question-blind captions} drop what the question needs and the captioner did not write (stacked comparisons, reversals). Sonnet partly sees the stacked comparison on frames (0.66), and captions written with the question in view recover most of it (\S\ref{sec:aware}), so much of that loss comes from writing blind. They do not recover the reversals. Why a description of the frames loses a reversal that the same model partly sees in them remains open. The question-aware captions are written per question and are not an index. Whether a fixed index can keep these facts without knowing the question is untested beyond the aspect bullet.

\textbf{Future work.} Videos released after the readers' training cutoff, with answers made by editing them, would rule out contamination. A signal at answer time, such as two text answers that disagree \citep{readfirst}, may choose between index and frames where the aspect and the models do not. A task that asks which candidate fills a masked span would compare the modalities on reconstruction. Over 1\,s gaps, copying the adjacent frame is level with a text reader (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), but the missing twenty minutes of a wedding, with its vows and rings, may be easier to guess from script knowledge than from pixels. Long videos would show where captioning everything stops paying.

\paragraph{Caption prompt.} Every captioner gets the same prompt. For frames (Haiku, Sonnet, and Gemini's frame and frame-clip captions), the first sentence is:
\begin{lstlisting}
The attached images are {n} frames from a {d}-second video clip, taken one second apart and shown in time order. Describe what happens in the clip, in one or two sentences of at most 45 words in total.
\end{lstlisting}
For Gemini's video captions, it is ``Describe what happens in the attached 5-second video clip, in one or two sentences of at most 45 words in total.'' The rest is the same for all:
\begin{lstlisting}
* Start with what happens: what the people, animals or machines do, or, if nothing in the clip acts, how the scene or the camera view changes.
* Then add the details that set this moment apart: the key objects and any on-screen text, but do not transcribe subtitles, clocks, timestamps, dates or numbering (such as day, part or step numbers), and leave out watermarks and channel logos.
* Describe the setting in a few words at most.
* Mention camera movement only when the camera clearly moves.
* Describe what is seen, not what is heard.
* Include enough details so someone who hasn't seen the video could understand what's happening.
* If a description cannot be generated, write "ERROR".
\end{lstlisting}
The whole-video captions (\S\ref{sec:robust}) get all of a video's frames in one call, and the length clause scales with the number of windows (for two windows: ``in at most 4 sentences of at most 90 words in total''). The aspect-aware captions (\S\ref{sec:aware}) add one bullet before the last:
\begin{lstlisting}
* The description will be used later to answer questions about the actions, the speed of the movements, the direction of the movements, the order of the events, and changes in the attributes of things (such as size, colour, shape or state). State these whenever the frames show them.
\end{lstlisting}
The question-aware captions (\S\ref{sec:aware}) are per window, and add instead, before the last bullet:
\begin{lstlisting}
* The description will be used, together with descriptions of the other parts of the same video, to answer this question about the video: {stem} The options are: {A. option; B. option; ...}. Describe what these frames show that bears on the question.
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Reader prompt.}
\begin{lstlisting}
Answer a multiple-choice question about a video clip that is {duration} seconds long. There is no sound.

{modality}

{evidence}

Question: {stem}
{options}

Answer with JSON only, in this format: {"answer": "<letter>"}, with one of the letters {letters}.
\end{lstlisting}
The evidence is the frames or the captions, each window labelled with its time span (``0-5 s:''). The modality sentence is, for frames:
\begin{lstlisting}
The video was split into windows of 5 seconds (the last one may be shorter). Each window is shown as its frames taken one second apart, in time order.
\end{lstlisting}
for captions:
\begin{lstlisting}
The video was split into windows of 5 seconds (the last one may be shorter). Each window is given as a short description of what can be seen in it, in time order. Each description was written by an AI model that watched only that one window, without sound, and knew nothing about the rest of the video.
\end{lstlisting}
for whole-video captions:
\begin{lstlisting}
The video is given as a single description of what can be seen in it. The description was written by an AI model that watched the whole video, without sound.
\end{lstlisting}
for one frame per window, ``\ldots{} Each window is shown as one frame from its middle, in time order.''; and for the question only, ``You cannot see the video. Give your best guess from the question and options alone.''

\paragraph{Describe, then answer.} The reader prompt with frames, with the last paragraph replaced by:
\begin{lstlisting}
First describe each window in one sentence, in time order: what can be seen in it and what happens. Then answer. Answer with JSON only, in this format: {"windows": ["<window 1>", "<window 2>", ...], "answer": "<letter>"}, with one description per window and one of the letters {letters}.
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Audit judge (\S\ref{sec:audit}).}
\begin{lstlisting}
You are checking whether written descriptions of a video contain the answer to a question about it.

The video is {duration} seconds long, without sound. It was split into windows of 5 seconds (the last one may be shorter). An AI model wrote one description per window, seeing only that one window and not the question:

{captions}

Question: {stem}
{options}

The correct answer is {answer}. {answer_text}

Judge the descriptions only, not what you think the video shows. Choose one label:
- "correct": the descriptions state the correct answer, or imply it clearly enough that a careful reader would choose it.
- "wrong": the descriptions state, or clearly imply, a different option.
- "silent": the descriptions do not decide between the options: the fact needed is missing or too vague.

Answer with JSON only, in this format: {"label": "<correct|wrong|silent>", "evidence": "<a short quote from the descriptions, or an empty string>"}
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Development phase.} The hypotheses came from 100 development questions (20 per aspect, one per video, on videos the confirmatory set does not use), read by Haiku (question only, Gemini video captions, 1 and 5 frames per window) and Sonnet (Gemini video captions and 5 frames), and on 50 of them by Haiku with its self-captions. \section{Amendments, deviations and term mapping}
\label{app:amend}

\paragraph{Terms.} The pre-registration called the modality effect the \emph{format} effect, and the source effect \emph{coverage}; they were renamed before any later entry, and the pre-registered entries keep their words. C1's coverage term (S2: Gemini video − frame captions) mixes the source with a 17× difference in visual tokens, so the paper's source effect is C2c, at about matched tokens.

\paragraph{Amendments, each made before the calls it affects.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sampling (C1).} The first sampling rule (aspects filled rarest first) reached only 59 action and 59 direction questions and wrote no file; a preference for videos with a single aspect was added, and the sample was drawn once.
\item \textbf{Gemini image cost (C1).} Gemini encodes each 224\,px image at ≈ 1,085 tokens at its default media resolution, not the assumed ≈ 66, so a frame-caption call cost about 3× the planned amount; the spend cap was raised and the resolution kept, so the captioner settings stayed as tested.
\item \textbf{Exclusions (C1).} Gemini returned no caption for 4 of 1,544 windows (0.26\%, below the 2\% stop rule): both windows of one video, blocked as prohibited content, and one window in each of two two-clip videos, where the window spans the cut and the frame captioner answered ``ERROR''. The 3 affected questions (all order) were dropped from every arm, leaving 297. Two of them are two-clip videos, so the exclusion slightly favours the frame captioner; at 3 of 300 questions it cannot move an estimate by more than 0.01.
\item \textbf{Stop rule (C1).} The Claude quota rule stopped Sonnet's secondary arms (question only and 1 frame) after C1's main arms. Sonnet's question-only arm was run later as C8; Sonnet on 1 frame was never run.
\item \textbf{Token checks (C2, C10).} C2's check expected ≈ 330 Gemini tokens per window and got 280 on split-screen videos; the video arm got the same counts for the same windows, so the rule was restated as ``equal to the video arm's tokens'' and held for 693 of 761 windows (the rest are short last windows, +2\% in total). C10's check omitted a fixed cost of 3 tokens per image block; all three checked calls fit the corrected count exactly.
\item \textbf{Predictions by type (C3).} Amendment~1 added by-type predictions for C3, after the exploratory by-type analysis of C1 and C2 and before any C3 call.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deviations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Haiku did not keep to the 45-word limit: on the 761 confirmatory windows its captions have a median of 45 words and 47\% are over it, against 16\% for Sonnet, 10\% for Qwen and none for Gemini. The most useful captions (Gemini's) are the shortest, so length does not explain the captioner effect.
\item The development entries K1 and K2 were written before their runs but committed after them.
\item The open reader (C12) differs from the Claude readers by design: greedy decoding without retry (an unparsed answer counts as wrong), Qwen's default system prompt, the content blocks joined into one message, no thinking, and no cost account.
\item The rule for the development step K1 did not say what to do when its second stage was also ambiguous; it was, and the index direction was continued without claiming a tie.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Hand labels.} The labels were blind to the judge, but made in a session where Claude (Opus) pointed out caption details on 6 items; κ is 0.81 with the first-instinct labels too.

\paragraph{More on cost.} Figure~\ref{fig:cost} plots accuracy against cost per question. Captions cut the video-content tokens to 43\% of the frames', but the input tokens only to 81\%. Sonnet on its self-captions repays after $\qbe = 8.9$ [7.8, 10.2], at −0.088. Haiku on Sonnet captions is +0.057 more accurate than Haiku on 448\,px frames and cheaper per question, and repays after 57.5 [53.3, 62.1]. Latency does not favour captions: the median call on captions is 0.1--1.1\,s slower than on frames with the same reader (CLI wall clock with start-up, not controlled for time of day). Thinking is 2--5\% of reader tokens.

\paragraph{Longer videos.} The clips average 2.6 windows, so the fixed reader prompt is a large share of each question's cost. We project the measured costs by Proposition~2: each arm's cost per question is its mean plus its input tokens per window (exact by construction: 200 for frames, 63--87 for captions) at its input price per extra window, and the index costs its mean per window times $W$. This assumes that output and thinking tokens do not grow with $W$, and that the reader reads every window. For one reader, the saving per question grows with length: Gemini video captions cost Haiku 0.82 of the frames' price per question at 2.6 windows, 0.59 at one minute (12 windows) and 0.38 at ten minutes (120), and Sonnet 0.70, 0.53 and 0.37. The break-even does not fall, because captioning grows with every window too: Haiku's \qbe{} stays at 203--214, and Sonnet's rises from 5.9 to a limit of 10.7 (at the measured 2.6 windows the projection gives 203 and 5.9, against the measured 202 and 5.8), since its fixed saving (less thinking on captions) counts for less on long videos. For the reader downgrade, the per-window indexes' \qbe{} of 1.8--3.7 rises to 4.5--8.9 at one minute and to a limit of 7.2--14.5, while the cost per question falls from 1/23--1/25 to 1/50 of Sonnet on frames. The limit does not depend on the reader's fixed prompt, including the CLI's. A reader that retrieves a few windows instead of reading all saves less per question, so for it these \qbe{} are lower bounds.

\paragraph{The projection against measured costs.} The EgoSchema development run (39 videos of 36 windows; Limitations) read every window with the same arms and prompts, so it measures the costs that Proposition~2 projects; contamination does not bear on costs. Its frames are 224×168, so each takes 48 image tokens, not 40. Projected from the TempCompass measurements with that correction (8 tokens per frame, for reads and for Claude's captioning), every arm's cost per question is within 11\% of the measured one (measured / projected 1.05--1.06 for frame arms, 1.04--1.11 for caption arms), the index cost per video within 2\%, and every \qbe{} within 7\%: 6.3 measured against 6.6 projected for Haiku on Sonnet's captions against Sonnet on frames, 11.3 against 11.4 for Sonnet on its captions, and 223 against 239 for Haiku on Sonnet's captions against Haiku on frames. The reads take 7--10\% more input tokens than projected, and output tokens differ by −41\% to +57\%, but output is under 120 tokens per read. Without the correction, measured \qbe{} is 0.70--0.84 of projected. The run's \qbe{} for the first pair, 6.3 [6.1, 6.4], was predicted in writing to fall in [5, 10]; the full comparison was written down before it was computed, after the run (research log A9).

\paragraph{Question-level and clustered CIs.} For every pair of the 28 arms on the confirmatory questions with any difference (378), we computed both 95\% CIs. The median ratio of their widths (clusters / questions) is 1.00 (IQR 0.95--1.05), since most of the 156 clusters hold one or two questions. In 6 pairs one CI excludes 0 and the other does not, each with an endpoint within 0.003 of 0. \section{The decision rule in detail}
\label{app:decide}

Three research-log entries, each committed before it ran: A6 (two scores as predictors, no calls), A7 (development of the rule, no calls) and A8 (the test, 2,952 Haiku calls).

\paragraph{In brief.} Haiku's self-captions win 34 of the questions where they and its frames disagree and lose 25, and Qwen on Sonnet's captions wins 26 and loses 25. A6's two scores recover how the videos were built (two-clip videos move most and lose least), and they add nothing to the video type. In A7, a selection rule fixed in advance chose aspect + pair. Alone, the pair ranks at 0.695 [0.640, 0.749] and the aspect at 0.602 [0.522, 0.673]. Within a pair, the rule ranks at 0.599 [0.510, 0.676] (computed after the run). For decisions, most of the rule's value is the choice per pair. Against the best fixed choice per pair it gains +0.005 [−0.003, +0.014] in accuracy, and its one signal within a pair is that speed questions favour the frames. In A8, we did not predict one shift. Haiku's frames score 0.699 on these questions against 0.613 on the confirmatory ones, mostly on reworded direction questions, and the number of options does not explain it (below). For a weak reader, the value of the index thus depends on the question set. The speed shift is P(index right | speed) − P(index right | other aspects) on disagreements, 0.39 against 0.54, and its sign holds for all three indexes. The rule's gain over always reading the index was predicted at +0.005.

\paragraph{A6: question-blind scores.} \emph{Motion} is the mean SigLIP~2 cosine distance between consecutive 224\,px frames, 1\,s apart, over the whole video. \emph{Coverage} is, per captioner, the mean over windows of the SigLIP~2 cosine between the window's caption (text tower \texttt{siglip2-base-patch16-224}, in the frames' space) and the mean of its frames. A gate run before any outcome was joined checked that each caption is closer to its own window than to a random window of another video: it is in every window, for both captioners. A loss is a question that a reader answers right from its 5 frames and wrong from its self-captions; the rows are the 419 (reader, question) pairs with the frames right (Haiku and Sonnet), with 63 losses. The statistic is a stratified AUC: over pairs of a loss and a kept answer from the same video type and reader, the share where the loss has the higher motion (lower coverage). By reader, motion gives 0.45 (Haiku) and 0.40 (Sonnet), coverage 0.45 and 0.59; on plain videos, 0.45 and 0.51 (17 losses); for gains instead of losses, 0.52 and 0.42; no CI excludes 0.5. By aspect (descriptive), motion cuts both ways: more motion means more losses on speed questions (0.64 [0.45, 0.78]) and fewer on direction questions (0.27 [0.11, 0.47]), where a slow reversed clip gives the caption little to commit a direction to. As classifiers (out-of-fold logistic regression, 10 folds by clip cluster), aspect + reader gives AUC 0.751, + motion and coverage 0.785, + video type instead 0.782, and both 0.794: the scores add what the video type adds, and nothing on top of it.

\paragraph{A7: development.} The rows are the questions where a reader's index and frames answers disagree, for 15 (reader, index) pairs: Haiku on its self-captions, Sonnet captions (224 and 448\,px) and Gemini video, frame and frame-clip captions; Sonnet on Sonnet captions (224 and 448\,px) and Gemini video captions; Qwen on its self-captions, Haiku's, Sonnet's and Gemini's three. Of 4,455 rows, 898 disagree, and the index is right on 513. The features are all known before the question is read: video (motion; cuts, $\log(1 + n)$ for the $n$ consecutive-frame distances above 0.20; the log number of windows), captions of this index (coverage; \emph{mention}, the share of windows whose caption uses a fixed word list for the question's aspect, e.g.\ for speed ``fast'', ``slowly'', ``gradually''; 0 for action questions), the task (aspect), and the pair (reader, captioner family). The models are L2 logistic regressions (C = 1). Table~\ref{tab:a7} gives their out-of-fold AUC; the selection rule (the highest AUC among M1--M5, or the smallest model within 0.01 of it) chose M1. Fit on all 898 rows, its standardized coefficients are: speed −0.57 [−0.93, −0.19] and order +0.30 [−0.04, +0.75] (against action); Sonnet reader −0.60 [−0.88, −0.32] and Qwen reader −0.41 [−0.67, −0.10] (against Haiku); Haiku, Qwen and Sonnet captioners −0.35, −0.53 and −0.22 against Gemini, every CI excluding 0. In decisions, it beats always reading the frames by +0.056 [+0.039, +0.074] and always reading the index by +0.028 [+0.015, +0.041], because it learns per pair which one is better; against that best fixed choice per pair it gains +0.005 [−0.003, +0.014], 0.08 of the headroom to an oracle. Its gains are on the pairs that are close to even: Qwen on Sonnet's captions +0.030 and Haiku's self-captions +0.024 over their best fixed choice.

\section{Minimal-pair construction}
\label{app:pairs}
For each confirmatory question, the sibling is the first video (sorted by ID) built from the same filmed clip that has a TempCompass question with the same aspect, stem and option set and a different correct option. Siblings are the plain and reversed versions of a clip, the other stacked versions (other speed pairs), and the other two-clip videos of the same two clips (other order or layout). Only siblings that Gemini had already captioned were used, which leaves out 3 reversal pairs. This gives 193 pairs: 107 reversal (66 with the reversed video in the confirmatory set, 41 with the plain one), 41 stacked and 45 two-clip, on 174 sibling videos (449 windows). Some siblings are development videos; each sibling item is still a new question--video combination. The sibling is read with the confirmatory question's wording and option order. Chance pair accuracy, both answers right by guessing, is 0.093.

Pair accuracies by group are in Table~\ref{tab:pairs}. By group (95\% CIs over clip clusters): on stacked pairs, Haiku on Sonnet's captions trails Sonnet on frames by −0.34 [−0.60, −0.08], and Sonnet on its self-captions trails its frames by −0.41 [−0.62, −0.23]; the same-answer rate is above Sonnet on frames' for Haiku on its self-captions (+0.32 [+0.13, +0.49]), Haiku on Sonnet's captions (+0.32 [+0.07, +0.55]) and Sonnet on its self-captions (+0.24 [+0.05, +0.43]), but not clearly for Haiku on Gemini's video captions (+0.22 [−0.02, +0.44]). On two-clip pairs, Haiku's self-captions beat its frames by +0.36 [+0.16, +0.56]. On reversal pairs, Sonnet's captions beat Haiku's self-captions by +0.06 [−0.01, +0.13], where a positive effect was predicted. Haiku on frames gives the same letter on both videos of a pair 54\% of the time, and on two-clip pairs its pair accuracy (0.18) is near chance.

\section{Further related work}
\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Readers on a fixed store.} In retrieval-augmented QA, 4.5--83.6\% of the gap between the weakest and the strongest reader survives a fixed compressed text across 13 panels \citep{compressneutral}. In agent memory, three readers tie on a graph store written by a weak model and spread out on one written by a strong model \citep{memorytax}, so a readers' tie is not automatic. Ours is on a strong captioner's index.

\paragraph{Text built for the task.} \citet{semserial} turn detector output into text in the task's own vocabulary with fixed code. A text-only model reading it beats a vision-language model of the same size on the image, and the advantage grows as the reader shrinks and reverses at the smallest size tested. The text is engineered for the decision, unlike a question-blind caption, but there too the sign depends on the reader.

\paragraph{What an index keeps.} \citet{inclusion} separate \emph{target inclusion}, whether the queried fact reaches the reader, from \emph{reader recovery}, whether the reader recovers a fact that is present. In these terms, the audit's omissions (\S\ref{sec:audit}) are failures of inclusion.

\paragraph{Routing.} \citet{readfirst} answer long-video questions from a memory of speech transcripts and captions, and look at the frames only when two derivations of the answer from the text disagree. With the gate held open, looking helps on the questions the gate would escalate and not on the rest. Their signal costs one more text call per question. Ours uses only what is known before reading, and we did not test answer-time signals. EARS \citep{ears} routes each video between frame selectors from motion and audio statistics, and, like our scores of motion and cuts, the statistics do not predict which choice is better.

\paragraph{Cost.} REEL \citep{reel} amortizes GPU time over a horizon of questions for one model, and MemoryTax \citep{memorytax} gives a break-even in questions for an agent memory at list prices, with one model writing and reading. Neither has a cheaper reader. For other work on cost, see \citet{mit3,remembr,lohi,vei,agentsmatter}.
